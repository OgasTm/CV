# IRSA early-stage development: phase/work-package decomposition, regulated-ready engineering practices and open-source stack (research notes, as of 2026-11-20)

Scope reminder: IRSA = BLE patient-worn dosimeter + smartphone agent app (contextual module, physics dose engine with optional MC-trained AI surrogate + conformal uncertainty + OOD fallback, versioned per-jurisdiction rules-as-code engine, LLM layer that only verbalises engine outputs, on-device privacy by default, no regulator reporting in v1). Planned phases from earlier analysis: 0 governance & architecture, 1 physics bench, 2 offline agent evaluation (IRSA-Bench), 3 clinical shadow mode, 4 intervention/regulatory pilot.

Method note: web searches + primary-page fetches; licences were checked directly against LICENSE files on raw.githubusercontent.com or PyPI metadata on 2026-11-20 (GitHub API was not reachable). Where only secondary sources (vendor blogs, law-firm notes) were available, this is flagged.

## Q1. Medical-device software lifecycle requirements to build in from the prototype stage (IEC 62304, ISO 14971, IEC 62366-1, IEC 82304-1, ISO 13485-lite, IEC 81001-5-1, AI guidance), minimal design history, open templates

### Takeaway
The standards set that applies today is still IEC 62304:2006+A1:2015, ISO 14971:2019 (plus BS/AAMI 34971:2023 for ML), IEC 62366-1, EN IEC 81001-5-1:2022, and on the AI side IMDRF GMLP N88 (final, Jan 2025), FDA's final PCCP guidance (Dec 2024), FDA's AI lifecycle guidance (still a draft from Jan 2025) and MDCG 2025-6. The EU AI Act high-risk obligations for MDR devices now apply from **2 Aug 2028** (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744). IEC 62304 edition 2 is **not** published and will probably not appear before 2027–2029. A small academic team can stay "regulation-ready" by keeping a light, git-native design history (intended use, requirements, architecture + SOUP list, risk/hazard log, verification records, data/model documentation) from Phase 0. They should **not** reuse OpenRegulatory's current templates in a future commercial spin-off without care, because the repository is now CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. An older fork is CC BY 4.0.

### Cited Findings

**EU AI Act / MDR interplay and timeline**
- MDCG 2025-6 (FAQ on the MDR/IVDR and AI Act interplay) was jointly adopted by the MDCG and the AI Board and published 19 June 2025 — [European Commission](https://health.ec.europa.eu/latest-updates/mdcg-2025-6-faq-interplay-between-medical-devices-regulation-vitro-diagnostic-medical-devices-2025-06-19_en); [OpenRegulatory summary](https://openregulatory.com/mdcg/mdcg-2025-6)
- MDCG 2025-6 says MDR class IIa/IIb/III devices that are AI systems are high-risk under AI Act Art. 6(1) because a notified body is involved. The AI Act does not raise the MDR class. Conformity assessment follows the MDR procedure. Manufacturers are encouraged to fold the AI Act QMS elements into the MDR QMS and keep one set of technical documentation. An MDR clinical investigation also counts as AI Act "real-world testing" (Art. 60) — [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/mdcg-published-new-guidance-on-the-interplay-between-the-mdr-ivdr-and-the-ai-act); [NSF summary](https://www.nsf.org/za/en/life-science-regulatory-news/mdcg-2025-6-eu-commission-publishes-faq-on-interplay-between-mdr-ivdr-and-ai-act)
- The Digital Omnibus on AI is **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744**, published in the OJ on 24 July 2026 and in force since 27 July 2026. Obligations for Annex I products (incl. MDR/IVDR) move from 2 Aug 2027 to **2 Aug 2028**, and stand-alone Annex III systems move to 2 Dec 2027. Notified bodies already designated under MDR/IVDR must apply for AI Act designation by **28 Jan 2028**. Art. 50 transparency obligations have applied since 2 Aug 2026. MDR/IVDR remain in Annex I Section A. A separate Commission proposal (16 Dec 2025) to move them to Section B is still under negotiation — [QLE Regulatory Brief, 3 Aug 2026](https://www.qle.be/the-qle-regulatory-brief/ai-acts-digital-omnibus-is-now-law-ai-medical-devices-get-until-2-august-2028-but-notified-bodies-face-a-28-january-2028-deadline); [EUR-Lex reference as cited by QLE](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng). Note: some secondary sources disagree on the exact Parliament/Council vote dates, but all agree on the July 2026 entry into force — [aiacto](https://www.aiacto.eu/en/blog/digital-omnibus-ai-act-report-2027); [healthydata.science](https://healthydata.science/ai-regulations-news-today-eu-ai-omnibus-delays-medical-device-ai-rules-until-2028/)

**IEC 62304 status**
- In force: IEC 62304:2006 (+2008 corrigendum) and IEC 62304:2015 (Amd 1, "version 1.1"). The 2015 consolidated version is described as not yet harmonised under the MDR but state of the art. The 2025 CD1 for edition 2 received almost 1,500 comments. As of 8 July 2026 the working group (SC62A MT49) planned a **CD2**, not the final edition. FDIS is not expected before 2028, so publication may slip to 2029 (IEC forecast was Oct 2028) — [Johner Institute, 8 Jul 2026](https://blog.johner-institute.com/iec-62304-medical-software/iec-62304-2nd-edition-all-areas-of-application-and-changes/)
- Draft edition 2 content: scope widened to "health software" (absorbing the role of IEC 82304-1-type products). Process rigour is "commensurate" with risk, and the 2025 CD replaced safety classes A/B/C with two "software process rigour levels" (I/II). Security risk management is explicit. Usability engineering is required, and AI process planning was added for AI/ML — [Johner](https://blog.johner-institute.com/iec-62304-medical-software/iec-62304-2nd-edition-all-areas-of-application-and-changes/); [NSF](https://www.nsf.org/ie/en/life-science-news/iec-62304-draft-of-the-2nd-edition-optimized-safety-classes-extended-scope-for-healthcare-software-and-ai)
- Conflict: one vendor (Critical Software) reported that FDIS began in May 2026 with publication early–mid 2027. VDE said the next CD was expected Aug 2026, which agrees with Johner — [Critical Software](https://asd.criticalsoftware.com/en/newsroom/iec-62304-edition-2-changes-august-2026); [VDE](https://www.vde.com/topics-en/health/expertise/iec-62304-edition-2-changes). Treat edition 2 as unpublished and its date as uncertain.
- When determining the software safety class under the current edition, the probability of software failure must be assumed to be 1 (often overlooked) — [Johner](https://blog.johner-institute.com/iec-62304-medical-software/iec-62304-2nd-edition-all-areas-of-application-and-changes/)

**QMS-lite, design history, and templates**

### Inferences
- **Safety class for IRSA (current 62304):** the dose engine and rules engine produce guidance that patients and caregivers act on (distance and time restrictions). A wrong output could lead to unnecessary radiation exposure of caregivers, children or pregnant contacts. With the failure probability assumed to be 1, Class **B** is the realistic floor. Class C is arguable if the hazard analysis concludes that a plausible software failure could contribute to serious injury (e.g., pregnant contact exposure), unless external risk controls (hospital written instructions, the dosimeter's own alarm) are credited. Decide this in Phase 0 and record the rationale in the risk file. (From domain knowledge of IEC 62304: Class B/C add software architecture documentation, unit-level verification and SOUP requirements. Class C adds detailed design per unit. Verify against the IEC 62304:2015 text.)
- **The minimal "design history" a research prototype should keep (git-native, roughly 10 living artefacts):** (1) intended-use / intended-purpose statement + user profiles (feeds MDR Annex II, the IEC 62366-1 use specification and AI Act Art. 13); (2) software development plan, 2–3 pages, naming the process rigour/safety class; (3) user needs and software requirements list with IDs (in markdown or issues) traced to tests; (4) architecture description with a SOUP/OTS list that records version, licence and known anomalies, incl. on-device LLM/ML models; (5) risk management file: ISO 14971 hazard log + BS/AAMI 34971 ML hazards (dataset shift, OOD, overtrust) + LLM hazards (hallucinated numbers, prompt injection from map text) + an 81001-5-1 threat model; (6) data management plan and dataset datasheets: provenance, train/test independence and reproducibility per GMLP; (7) model cards for the surrogate (training MC configuration, conformal calibration set, OOD thresholds); (8) verification records generated by CI (pytest reports, golden-case regression for the dose and rule engines, IRSA-Bench results), archived per release tag; (9) usability records: formative study protocols/results and a known use-error list; (10) release notes with residual anomalies, plus a change log via signed commits/PRs. This matches the Hauschild minimal set and lets the team later "lift" artefacts into a full QMS instead of writing them retrospectively.
- **QMS-lite:** an academic team does not need ISO 13485 certification for Phases 0–3. It needs documented procedures for document control, design control, risk management, SOUP management, problem resolution and configuration management. Under MDR Art. 62/82 the sponsor still has obligations, and an in-house (Art. 5(5)) route would require an "appropriate QMS" (see Q2). Plan certification only when a spin-off or industrial partner becomes the legal manufacturer.
- **AI Act timing is favourable but not a reason to defer:** with Annex I obligations from 2 Aug 2028, a CE submission around 2028–2029 would need AI Act-compliant documentation (data governance, logging, human oversight, accuracy/robustness). Building GMLP-style data/model documentation and automatic event logging into the prototype now avoids rework.
- **Use FDA's PCCP concept internally:** pre-specify which surrogate-model retraining and rule-pack updates are "planned changes", with a verification protocol. This maps onto the versioned rules-as-code design (rule packs as data with regression tests) and onto the MDR significant-change discussion later.
- **Template strategy:** use the CC BY 4.0 OpenSaMD snapshot (or write your own) for anything a future commercial entity will inherit. Use current OpenRegulatory CC BY-NC-SA material only for internal academic learning, and keep it out of any deliverable that a company would reuse.

### Gaps
- I did not confirm whether EN IEC 81001-5-1 and IEC 62304:2015/A1 are cited in the OJEU as harmonised under the MDR. Check the latest Commission implementing decisions.
- I found no reliable source on the status of IEC 82304-1 edition 2 or IEC 62366-1 revisions in 2026.
- ISO/IEC 42001:2023 (AI management system) relevance was not researched in depth. I found no source showing that notified bodies require it for MDR devices. Treat it as optional and possibly useful for AI Act QMS alignment (unverified).
- The text of IEC 62304's class-specific documentation obligations was not retrieved (paywalled). Verify the inferences above against the standard.

## Q2. Clinical-investigation pathway for a software prototype in an observational "shadow mode" study (EU MDR Art. 82 / ISO 14155, ethics, CE marking; US IDE/NSR)

### Takeaway
A shadow-mode study in which IRSA runs alongside standard care and its outputs are compared with caregiver TLDs and clinician judgement, without being shown to patients or acting on care, is in the EU most likely an MDR **Art. 82** investigation (not for conformity assessment) governed by national law and an ethics committee opinion. The device does **not** need CE marking for this. However, if the data are meant to support the later CE dossier, the full Art. 62 regime applies. The in-house exemption (Art. 5(5)) does **not** fit a patient-facing app used at home. ISO 14155 was revised (4th edition, March 2026). In the US, a non-invasive observational software study with no effect on care is a classic candidate for a **non-significant-risk (NSR)** determination under abbreviated IDE requirements with IRB oversight.

### Cited Findings
- Art. 82 covers clinical investigations not carried out for the Art. 62(1) purposes, and Member States set additional requirements. Finland's regulator applies Art. 82 where results will not feed the conformity assessment, e.g. investigator-led research or non-CE-marked pilots — [Fimea](https://fimea.fi/en/medical-devices/investigations-with-devices/clinical-evaluation-and-clinical-investigations); [BfArM](https://www.bfarm.de/EN/Medical-devices/Tasks/Clinical-investigations-and-performance-studies/Clinical-investigations/Notification-other/_node.html)
- UK MHRA guidance (GB, not EU, but instructive): retrospective or non-interventional software studies using existing data, with no patient interaction and no effect on patient management, may not count as a clinical investigation. Devices under investigation should not carry a CE/UKCA mark. An ethics opinion is required for investigations in scope — [MHRA](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/medical-devices-that-need-a-clinical-investigation/determining-if-a-clinical-investigations-is-required); [MHRA investigator guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/clinical-investigations-investigators-responsibilities/guidance-for-clincal-investigators)
- MDCG 2025-6: a clinical investigation under the MDR also counts as AI Act real-world testing (Art. 60) — [Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/mdcg-published-new-guidance-on-the-interplay-between-the-mdr-ivdr-and-the-ai-act)
- **In-house exemption (MDCG 2023-1, primary text read):** use within a health institution can be "physical or, for instance for medical device software, remote", but "if, during the lifecycle of the device, the device is used outside the health institution's legal entity, it cannot be in-house." The listed examples of devices that do **not** fall under in-house: "Medical device applications where patients can use the application outside the health institution, e.g. by entering or accessing medical data that are subsequently used by the healthcare professional." RUO products used for research purposes only are not regulated as in-house devices. Conditions include GSPR compliance, an appropriate QMS, justification that no equivalent CE device meets the need, a public declaration, documentation, vigilance, and non-industrial scale — [MDCG 2023-1](https://health.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2023-01/mdcg_2023-1_en.pdf)
- **ISO 14155:** the 4th edition was published 23 March 2026 and replaces the 2020 edition. Changes: required residual-risk assessment, which separates device-use risks from risks of procedures outside routine care; a new section on Clinical Events Committees; sponsors must justify not having a DMC; tighter eligibility-deviation and consent rules; clarified AE reporting and suspension; stronger statistics/endpoint requirements aligned with ICH E9(R1); new informative Annex K on investigation design — [ISO](https://www.iso.org/standard/14155); [NSF](https://www.nsf.org/life-science-regulatory-news/the-fourth-edition-of-iso-14155-was-published-on-march-23-2026-and-replaces-the-2020-version); [ANSI blog](https://blog.ansi.org/ansi/iso-14155-2026-clinical-investigation/); [Swissmedic](https://www.swissmedic.ch/swissmedic/en/home/medical-devices/klinische-versuche/mitteilungen-klv-mep/klinische-pruefungen-mep.html)
- ISO 14155 notes that for SaMD its requirements apply as far as relevant to showing analytical, scientific and clinical validity. Post-market investigations follow it "as far as relevant" (Annex I) — [iTeh preview of EN ISO 14155](https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/cen/ca6772f5-d1ab-4f7b-b82a-c94642a7ea73/en-iso-14155-2020-a11-2024)
- EN ISO 14155:2020/A11:2024 is listed as withdrawn (Apr/May 2026, dates differ by source) and replaced by EN ISO 14155:2026. Per the Estonian standards body, the withdrawn version keeps presumption of conformity under the MDR until the OJ is amended — [EVS](https://www.evs.ee/en/evs-en-iso-14155-2020-a11-2025); [Genorma](https://genorma.com/en/standards/en-iso-14155-2020-a11-2024)
- **US:** under 21 CFR 812.2(c) some device studies are IDE-exempt (e.g., legally marketed devices used per labelling, and certain non-invasive diagnostics not used without confirmation). A non-exempt NSR study needs only abbreviated IDE requirements and is overseen solely by the IRB. The sponsor makes the initial SR/NSR determination, and FDA is final arbiter if sponsor and IRB disagree — [UCSF](https://compliance.ucsf.edu/your-study-ide-exempt-or-non-significant-risk-nsr); [UPenn IRB](https://irb.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/Abbreviated-IDE-Requirements-US.pdf); [VCU decision tree](https://research.vcu.edu/media/office-of-research-and-innovation/reg-affairs/_Non-significantriskvssignificantriskdeterminationdecisiontree(24).pdf)
- IDE regulations apply to software functions under FDA oversight when the study objective is to assess the function's safety and/or effectiveness. FDA exercises enforcement discretion for some patient self-management software — [NIH Clinical Center](https://www.cc.nih.gov/node/1908)
- FDA revised its **Clinical Decision Support** and **General Wellness** guidances on 6 Jan 2026, superseding the 2022 CDS and 2019 wellness versions. The CDS guidance reflects a broader reading of the 520(o)(1)(E) carve-out (e.g., it deleted the 2022 statement that "may exhibit signs" outputs fail Criterion 3) — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2026/01/fda-updates-its-clinical-decision-support-and-general-wellness-guidances-key-points); [FDA Law Blog](https://www.thefdalawblog.com/2026/01/a-busy-day-in-the-cdrh-neighborhood-updates-to-the-cds-and-general-wellness-guidance-documents/); [King & Spalding](https://www.kslaw.com/insights/articles/fda-updates-general-wellness-and-clinical-decision-support-guidance-documents)

### Inferences
- **Design Phase 3 as "silent shadow mode":** patients wear the dosimeter, the app runs and logs engine outputs, but advice is hidden from patients and caregivers (or shown only to the research team). Standard written discharge instructions continue. Caregiver TLDs/EPDs are ground truth. This minimises the device's influence on care. In some Member States it could even fall outside "clinical investigation" if no device output affects subjects, but the wearable dosimeter is itself an investigational component, so plan for an Art. 82 + ethics route anyway. Confirm with the national competent authority (e.g., BfArM/Swissmedic/Fimea, depending on site).
- **Choose Art. 62 vs Art. 82 deliberately:** if Phase 3/4 data are to be used as clinical evidence in the CE technical file, run them under Art. 62 from the start: sponsor, CIP per ISO 14155:2026, investigator's brochure, GSPR checklist, competent-authority notification. From MDR text (domain knowledge; verify): Art. 70(7) lets class I and non-invasive class IIa/IIb investigations start after validation unless national law says otherwise, subject to a favourable ethics opinion. Art. 82 is cheaper, but its data may carry less weight for conformity assessment.
- **Engineering consequence:** the investigational software must be version-locked (frozen build, hash), with a documented risk analysis, verification report and IFU-like user documentation for investigators. This is why the Phase 0–2 design history matters. ISO 14155:2026 also needs AE/device-deficiency reporting flows, so a deficiency report button in the app plus an audit log is useful.
- **No CE marking for shadow mode:** investigational devices are not CE-marked. Label "exclusively for clinical investigation".
- **US (if a US site is planned):** document an NSR rationale (non-invasive; observational; no change to care; advice not acted on). Get IRB concurrence under abbreviated IDE (labelling, IRB approval, informed consent, monitoring, records). The Jan 2026 CDS guidance might place some HCP-facing functions outside device regulation, but the patient-facing radiation-safety advice is unlikely to qualify as non-device CDS (the carve-out targets HCPs). Treat it as a device function (inference).
- **AI Act Art. 60:** since an MDR investigation also counts as real-world testing, there is no need for a separate AI Act sandbox procedure for Phase 3.

### Gaps
- Member-State-specific Art. 82 rules (Germany MPDG, Austria, Switzerland ClinO-MD, China/IAEA partner countries) were not compiled. The site country matters a lot.
- I found no EU-level guidance specific to "shadow-mode" AI software studies. MDCG 2021-6 (Q&A on clinical investigations) was not fetched.
- National radiation-protection law implications (e.g., caregiver dose constraints under Euratom BSS 2013/59 implementation) were out of scope here.

## Q3. Reference architectures for safety-critical health agents in 2025–2026 (MCP tool calling, CaMeL control/data separation, typed outputs, verifiers, audit logging; industry examples)

### Takeaway
Current patterns all point to the same design: a deterministic orchestrator owns control flow. Tools (engines) are exposed via typed schemas, e.g. MCP tools with `outputSchema`/`structuredContent`. Untrusted text, such as map/POI text or user free text, is processed by a quarantined model with no tool access, as in CaMeL. Every output is verified before display, and every step is traced. CHAI's July 2026 agentic guide explicitly says many workflows should be deterministic "skills" rather than agents. That supports IRSA's design: engines decide, the LLM verbalises.

### Cited Findings
- **CaMeL** (Debenedetti et al., Google DeepMind, arXiv 2503.18813): a protective system layer around the LLM. A privileged LLM writes a plan from the trusted user query. A quarantined LLM processes untrusted data with no tool access. A custom Python interpreter tracks provenance via capabilities (tags on each value) and enforces policies before each tool call. On AgentDojo, a later version reports 77% of tasks solved with provable security vs 84% undefended (v1: 67%) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.18813); [Simon Willison summary](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Apr/11/camel/). Reference code is **Apache-2.0** — [google-research/camel-prompt-injection](https://github.com/google-research/camel-prompt-injection)
- **MCP spec history:** structured tool output (`outputSchema`/`structuredContent`) arrived in **2025-06-18** — [2025-06-18 changelog](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/changelog). **2025-11-25** added incremental scope consent, tool-name guidance, input-validation errors returned as tool-execution errors (so models can self-correct), experimental tasks, and URL-mode elicitation — [2025-11-25 changelog](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-11-25/changelog)

### Inferences
- **Proposed IRSA reference architecture (maps cleanly onto the cited patterns):**
  1. *Orchestrator (deterministic state machine, not an LLM)* owns the dialogue state and the slot-filling plan. It asks engine-driven clarification questions (e.g., "do you share a bed?" when the rule engine needs that slot).
  2. *Tools as typed services:* `dose_engine.predict(...)`, `rules.evaluate(jurisdiction, version, facts)`, `half_life.update(...)`, `dosimeter.read()`. Expose them in-process. Optionally use MCP (2026-07-28 stateless profile) if a server boundary is needed, with `outputSchema` and units in the schema. Use OpenTelemetry trace propagation for audit.
  3. *Quarantined LLM* (CaMeL idea): the only component that reads untrusted text (map POI names, free-text patient replies). It may only emit typed facts (enum/number with provenance tag `untrusted`), never instructions or tool calls.
  4. *Verbaliser LLM:* receives only verified engine outputs as typed slots and renders patient-friendly text in the target language.
  5. *Output verifier:* deterministic checks that every number, unit, duration and restriction in the text equals a slot value (regex/AST extraction + comparison). It also checks for forbidden content (no new medical advice, no dose numbers not in slots) and reading level. On failure it falls back to a template rendering. That fallback must always exist and is the "safe state".
  6. *Audit log:* append-only, on-device, hash-chained event log of inputs, engine versions, rule-pack version, model hashes, verifier verdicts and displayed text. It supports AI Act logging (Art. 12) and ISO 14155 source data later.
- Treat the LLM verbaliser as a component whose failure is detectable by the verifier. This is the strongest argument for keeping the LLM out of the safety-critical path in the risk file. It may also allow a lower process rigour for that item if the architecture segregates it (IEC 62304 segregation argument; verify with the notified body).
- CHAI's "consent-scope object travels with data" maps to IRSA's privacy-by-default: tag every datum with purpose and consent scope and block export (FHIR) unless the scope allows it.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve full CHAI guide text on audit logging or tool permissions (the blog page does not state them), and the licence of the CHAI T&E framework repo was not found.
- I found no Anthropic- or OpenAI-specific "healthcare agent reference architecture" document in this session. Only general API features were verified.
- No independent evaluation of CaMeL's guarantees in health settings was found.

## Q4. Mobile and BLE stack, background-BLE limits, on-device inference / small LLMs (2026), FHIR export

### Takeaway
For a medical-grade BLE streaming app, the safest choice is **native BLE layers** (CoreBluetooth on iOS; Android BLE via the companion-device APIs and a `connectedDevice` foreground service) behind a shared core. Kotlin Multiplatform with Kable (Apache-2.0) or Flutter with `flutter_reactive_ble` (BSD) are good cross-platform options. **Avoid `flutter_blue_plus` for anything that may go commercial:** since 2025 its licence requires paid licences for for-profit use, including development, and it sends build-time telemetry. Background BLE works on both OSes but with hard limits: iOS will not relaunch a force-quit app unless AccessorySetupKit is used (iOS 26+), and Android requires typed foreground services. On-device LLMs (Apple Foundation Models, Gemini Nano via ML Kit GenAI, or your own model via LiteRT-LM/ExecuTorch/llama.cpp/MLC) are viable for verbalisation only. Hospital export should use FHIR R4 (IPS/EPS-aligned), since R6 is not final.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- **Recommended mobile split:** a shared Kotlin Multiplatform core (domain model, dose/rule engines compiled or ported, audit log, FHIR mapping) with native UI (SwiftUI/Compose), or Compose Multiplatform. BLE via Kable plus thin native shims for AccessorySetupKit (iOS) and CompanionDeviceManager (Android). If the team prefers Flutter, use `flutter_reactive_ble` (BSD), not flutter_blue_plus. A university is exempt under the FBP licence, but a later spin-off would need a commercial licence for all development. The build-time telemetry ping would also need to appear in the DPIA.
- **Engines should be portable, deterministic code** (e.g., Kotlin/Swift or Rust core, or a Python reference implementation with a ported mobile version cross-checked by shared golden test vectors). Keep the Python scientific stack for research and bench work (Phase 1–2) and port only the frozen inference path to mobile.
- **Dosimeter firmware/BLE profile:** design a GATT service with sequence numbers, timestamps, a device-side buffer (for gaps when the phone kills the app) and a battery/health characteristic. The app must tolerate hours-long gaps, and the half-life estimator must treat missing data explicitly. This is a direct mitigation for the iOS/Android background limits above.
- **On-device LLM role:** use it only for verbalisation and the quarantined parsing of untrusted text, behind the deterministic verifier. Always keep a template fallback, because system models are device-gated: Apple Intelligence hardware only, and Gemini Nano only on supported Android devices. Bundling an open small model (e.g., Gemma-class via LiteRT-LM, or llama.cpp/MLC) gives version control (a model hash in the SOUP list) that system models do not.
- **FHIR:** export a clinician summary as FHIR R4 (Observation for measured dose rates, DocumentReference/Composition for the advice summary), aligned to IPS/EPS. Defer R6.

## Q6. Team composition, rough effort and timelines for academic digital-health device prototypes; proposed phase/WP decomposition

### Takeaway
Published sources give only qualitative guidance on academic SaMD teams and almost no hard effort data. Consultancy figures suggest MDR class IIa certification costs roughly €50–150k in external spend and adds 3–9 months of notified-body time once the technical file exists. The plan below is therefore an **inference** built from the regulatory requirements in Q1–Q2 and the engineering scope in Q3–Q5: roughly 8–12 FTE-years over about 30–36 months to reach the end of shadow mode, with go/no-go gates.

### Cited Findings
- The University of Manchester translation toolkit maps SaMD from basic research through concept to clinical testing and market. At concept stage it asks teams to formalise intended use and understand risk classification early, drawing on engineering, applied health research and clinical practice — [Manchester translation toolkit](https://translation-toolkit.manchester.ac.uk/roadmaps/software-medical-devices/)
- Academic groups are typically small teams with frequent turnover and mixed software-engineering backgrounds, working to tight schedules on proof-of-concept development. Hence the reduced IEC 62304 subset and the advice to give change management to permanent staff — [Hauschild et al., iScience 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9685384/)
- Cost and time estimates (consultancy, potentially self-interested): MDR class IIa/IIb roughly €50–80k (elsewhere "at least €30k, probably €50k"), with notified body €30–50k. Traditional consultants reportedly charge €120–150k for a class IIa software device. Class IIa takes about the same time as class I plus 3–9 months of notified-body audit wait, or 12+ months with traditional consultants — [OpenRegulatory cost](https://openregulatory.com/questions/how-much-does-medical-device-compliance-cost); [OpenRegulatory time](https://openregulatory.com/questions/how-long-does-medical-device-compliance-take); [OpenRegulatory consulting-as-a-service](https://openregulatory.com/articles/medical-device-compliance-10x-cheaper-consulting-as-a-service)

- An NIH SEED device regulatory case study (academic AI imaging) stresses early IRB authorisation and regulatory resources for commercialisation — [NIH SEED case study](https://seed.nih.gov/sites/default/files/2023-09/Device-Regulatory-Case-Study-1.pdf)

- ML engineer (surrogate, conformal, OOD, half-life), 1.0 FTE in Phases 1–2, 0.5 thereafter.
- Mobile/BLE engineer (+ firmware liaison), 1.0 FTE from Phase 1. Embedded engineer for the dosimeter, 0.5 FTE if the hardware is in-house.
- LLM/evaluation engineer (orchestrator, verifier, IRSA-Bench, red-teaming), 1.0 FTE in Phases 1–3.
- Regulatory/QA lead (QMS-lite, risk file, 62304/14971/62366/81001-5-1, DPIA with the DPO), 0.3–0.5 FTE, possibly shared institutional staff or an external consultant.
- HCI/usability researcher (62366-1 formative studies, multilingual health literacy), 0.5 FTE.
- Clinical research nurse/coordinator (Phase 3 recruitment, TLD logistics, AE reporting), 0.5–1.0 FTE in Phases 3–4.
- Data protection officer / information security, ad hoc.

**Phase 0 (months 0–4), Governance & architecture.** WPs: intended-use statement and claims matrix (what IRSA will and will not do); preliminary MDR classification (Rule 11 analysis) and AI Act high-risk determination; DPIA (GDPR Art. 35) and a privacy architecture (on-device default, no cloud PHI); development plan + safety class decision; risk management plan + initial hazard log (14971 + 34971 + LLM + 81001-5-1 threat model); SOUP/licence register (Q5 table); rules-as-code schema and first jurisdiction pack (e.g., one EU country + one IAEA-partner country) with citations; repo/CI/DVC/MLflow scaffolding. Deliverables: D0.1 Intended Use & Claims, D0.2 DPIA, D0.3 Architecture & SOUP, D0.4 Risk Plan + Hazard Log v0, D0.5 Rule Pack v0.1 + test vectors. **Gate G0:** intended use frozen at v1; no "unacceptable" hazard without a mitigation concept; DPIA shows on-device processing feasible; licence register clean (no AGPL/BUSL in the shipped path).

**Phase 1 (months 3–12), Physics bench.** WPs: semi-analytic model implementation; GATE 10 MC campaign (131I and 177Lu phantoms, geometries, shielding); surrogate training with conformal calibration and OOD detector; physical phantom measurements with reference survey meters/EPDs; dosimeter characterisation (energy response, linearity, angular response, BLE packet integrity); three-model comparison (semi-analytic vs surrogate vs MC vs measurement). Deliverables: D1.1 Physics verification report, D1.2 Surrogate model card + coverage report, D1.3 Dosimeter characterisation report. **Gate G1:** a pre-registered accuracy target versus phantom measurements (e.g., agreement within a set percentage at relevant distances, chosen in Phase 0); empirical conformal coverage at or above nominal on held-out geometries; OOD fallback triggers on predefined OOD cases.

**Phase 2 (months 9–18), Offline agent evaluation (IRSA-Bench).** WPs: orchestrator + typed tools + verifier + template fallback; IRSA-Bench scenario grammar (households, isotopes, languages, adversarial map text, ambiguous answers, red flags); metrics (slot fidelity, numeric-hallucination rate after the verifier = 0 target, clarification appropriateness, readability, injection resistance, escalation recall); a formative usability round (62366-1) with patients and caregivers; on-device LLM vs template comparison. Deliverables: D2.1 IRSA-Bench v1 (open release, CC BY/Apache), D2.2 Agent verification report, D2.3 Formative usability report, D2.4 Frozen investigational build + verification evidence. **Gate G2:** zero unverified numeric outputs reaching the UI in the benchmark; injection suite passes; usability shows no critical use errors on hazard-related tasks; the risk file shows residual risks acceptable for an observational study.

**Phase 3 (months 18–30), Clinical shadow mode.** WPs: CIP per ISO 14155:2026 (or the national Art. 82 requirements) + ethics + competent-authority notification as required; site set-up and training; recruitment (131I thyroid and 177Lu cohorts); caregiver TLD/EPD logistics; data monitoring and AE/device-deficiency handling; analysis (agreement of predicted vs measured caregiver dose, calibration of intervals, rule-engine concordance with clinician instructions, app reliability including BLE gap statistics). Deliverables: D3.1 CIP + ethics approvals, D3.2 Clinical investigation report, D3.3 Updated risk file/PMS-like learnings. **Gate G3:** predefined agreement and calibration endpoints met; no serious device-related incidents; BLE data completeness above a threshold; decide on the regulatory route (legal manufacturer, notified body, AI Act readiness for 2028).

**Phase 4 (months 30+), Intervention / regulatory pilot.** Out of the early-development scope. It requires a legal manufacturer with ISO 13485, a notified-body engagement plan, and the AI Act Annex I obligations applicable from 2 Aug 2028.

## Q7. Practical risks for early development: BLE background restrictions, App Store / Google Play health-app rules, GDPR/data residency for cloud LLM APIs, LLM API cost vs self-hosting

### Takeaway
The biggest practical risks are: (1) iOS force-quit and Android FGS rules causing dose-data gaps, mitigated by device-side buffering and AccessorySetupKit/CompanionDeviceManager; (2) store policies that demand regulatory proof for apps acting as medical devices, which means investigational builds should be distributed via TestFlight/closed testing or MDM rather than public store listings; (3) **Anthropic's first-party API offers no EU inference or storage geography** (only "us"/"global"), so EU-resident LLM processing requires Bedrock/Vertex EU endpoints, Azure EU Data Zones, or an on-device or self-hosted model; (4) per-conversation LLM API cost is small (cents) compared with the regulatory value of on-device processing.

### Cited Findings
**App stores**
- Apple App Review Guideline 1.4.1: medical apps that could give inaccurate data or be used to diagnose or treat patients may get greater scrutiny. Apps must disclose data and methodology behind health-measurement accuracy claims and hold the regulatory clearances they need. Guideline 5.1.3 bars using health/HealthKit data for advertising or data mining or disclosing it to third parties for those purposes, and 5.1.3(ii) reportedly bars storing personal health information in iCloud. Secondary trackers cite update dates of May, June and October 2026; I could not read Apple's page directly — [ConductAtlas (Apple 1.4.1 tracker)](https://conductatlas.com/platform/apple/apple-app-store-review-guidelines/healthcare-and-medical-app-requirements/); [Apple forum on 5.1.3](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/838022); [Apple guidelines PDF](https://developer.apple.com/support/downloads/terms/app-review-guidelines/App-Review-Guidelines-English-UK.pdf)
- Google Play: all developers, including those on closed/open testing tracks, must complete the Health apps declaration. Apps regulated as medical devices are labelled "Medical Device" and must give proof of approval, clearance or certification on request. Non-device health apps need a "not a medical device" disclaimer. Google is aligning the policy with MDCG guidance for EU-targeted apps, effective 28 Jan 2026 — [Google Play Health apps](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13996367); [Google Play health content](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14738291); [Google Play policy update](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511?hl=en)

**LLM data residency**
- Anthropic first-party API: `inference_geo` accepts only `"global"` (default) and `"us"`. Workspace geo (storage at rest) is only `"us"`. US-only inference costs 1.1x standard pricing on Claude 4.6+ models. Data residency is ZDR-eligible (except "Covered Models") and HIPAA-eligible. On Bedrock and Google-managed Vertex the region is set by the endpoint or inference profile, and on Microsoft Foundry only a US Data Zone is described — [Anthropic data residency docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/data-residency)
- Secondary sources: EU processing of Claude goes through Bedrock EU inference profiles (which may include the UK/Switzerland, so check the destination list) or the Vertex AI EU multi-region endpoint (reportedly GA since 15 May 2026). Azure OpenAI EU processing uses Data Zone Standard in an EU resource (not Global Standard). OpenAI's "Europe" region covers EEA + Switzerland, and system data may be processed outside the region. GDPR does not mandate EU-only processing per se; residency is a contractual/risk choice — [Cloudsail](https://cloudsail.com/blog/eu-data-residency-azure-openai-bedrock/); [Teamazing](https://www.teamazing.com/blog/claude-for-enterprise-eu-review-2026/); [Beri.net buyer's guide](https://www.beri.net/article/llm-data-residency-providers-in-region-processing-buyers-guide-2026). Flag: secondary, vendor-comparison blogs; verify against each provider's DPA and docs.

**LLM API pricing (Anthropic first-party, per 1M tokens, from the Claude API reference cached 2026-11-03; verify on the live pricing page)**
- Claude Haiku 5.5: $0.10 input / $0.50 output (prompts ≤100K tokens). Claude Sonnet 5.5: $2 / $10. Claude Opus 5.5: $4 / $20. Bedrock and Vertex have separate partner pricing — [Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)

### Inferences
- **Cost order of magnitude:** a typical IRSA verbalisation turn of about 2k input tokens (system prompt + typed slots, cacheable) and about 300 output tokens costs roughly $0.0004 on Haiku 5.5 or $0.007 on Sonnet 5.5. At about 50 turns per patient episode, that is roughly 2–35 cents per patient. API cost is negligible at research scale. The real cost drivers of a cloud LLM are DPIA/contract work, EU residency constraints (which rule out Anthropic's first-party API for EU-only processing), and vendor model churn (models retire, which forces re-verification under the change-control plan).
- **Self-hosting / on-device trade-off:** on-device verbalisation (system model or a bundled small model) gives the best privacy story and offline robustness, with no per-token cost. The downsides are device fragmentation and limited model choice. Self-hosting an open model in the hospital data centre gives version pinning and EU residency but adds GPU and ops costs (not quantified here). Recommendation for the prototype: (a) template renderer as the always-available baseline; (b) on-device model as primary; (c) optional EU-hosted cloud model only for IRSA-Bench comparisons on synthetic data. No real patient data goes to cloud LLMs in Phases 0–3.
- **Store distribution:** for Phase 3, distribute via TestFlight / Play closed testing or institutional MDM. The Google Play health declaration still applies to closed tracks. Avoid public listings until CE marking, because the stores may ask for regulatory proof for medical-device functionality.
- **BLE gap risk** should be a named hazard (loss of data, leading to a stale dose estimate and wrong advice). Mitigations: a device-side buffer, a staleness indicator in the UI, an engine refusing to give time-based advice when data are older than a threshold, and patient instructions not to force-quit, which should be tested in usability studies.
- **Licence/telemetry risk:** any SDK that phones home (e.g., flutter_blue_plus build-time ping, analytics SDKs) conflicts with privacy-by-default and must go through the DPIA and SOUP review.
