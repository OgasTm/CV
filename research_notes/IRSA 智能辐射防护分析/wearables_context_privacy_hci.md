# HCI, ubiquitous computing and mHealth evidence (2025–2026) for judging IRSA, the context-aware, location-using, wearable-driven radiation-safety patient assistant

Scope note: these notes cover human factors, alert design, sensor-grounded LLM agents, older/cancer-patient users, privacy-preserving sensing, crowd-data reliability and surveillance framing. Search date is 2026-11-19. Items marked **[secondary]** come from press or summary coverage, not the primary paper. Items marked **[title/abstract only]** were checked only at abstract level. **[unverified]** means I could not confirm the item in this session.

---

## 1. JITAIs, context-aware alerts, alert fatigue and micro-randomized trials (MRTs)

### Takeaway
In 2026 the JITAI evidence base is still "methodologically immature". Proximal effects of individual prompts are small or inconsistent, non-engagement with prompts is common, and almost no study measures notification burden directly. False alerts carry a measurable cost: in older adults, more than two false smartwatch alerts were linked to clinically meaningful drops in perceived physical health and self-management confidence. MRTs are the accepted way to test whether, when and how to alert, and 2026 methods work extends them to categorical (multi-variant) alerts.

### Cited Findings
- **2026 systematic review of JITAIs for physical activity** (Yang et al., *Frontiers in Public Health*, 4 Sep 2026; PROSPERO CRD420261432266) covers 37 studies with 6,939 adults: 18 RCTs, 7 MRTs, 2 SMARTs and other designs. Searches ran to 1 June 2026. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1934024/full)
  - Exploratory pooled effect on steps: **+534 steps/day (95% CI −79 to 1,147), I² = 85%**, so not significant and highly heterogeneous. No RCT showed a significant reduction in sedentary time (p = 0.07–0.24). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1934024/full)
  - 15 of 18 RCTs (83%) were rated moderate or high risk of bias. The authors call the evidence "methodologically immature" with "preliminary positive trends", and say the incremental value over static digital interventions "remains unclear". — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1934024/full)
  - Decision rules: 51.4% simple threshold/if-then rules and 45.9% "complex" context-aware or AI-driven rules. Only **1 of 37 used an LLM or generative AI**. 62.2% were fully automated. Wearable accelerometers supplied tailoring data in 54.1% of studies, and multi-source fusion (wearable + EMA + GPS) in 13.5%. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1934024/full)
  - Timing: fixed schedule 35.1%, event-triggered 24.3%, mixed 21.6%, random/adaptive 8.1%. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1934024/full)
  - **The review does not measure notification burden, fatigue over time or engagement decline.** It mentions "user fatigue" only as a design aim. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1934024/full)
  - MRT results across the 7 included MRTs: 4 showed positive proximal effects, e.g. Klasnja et al. 2019 HeartSteps found **+59 steps in the 30 min after a walking suggestion**, and Golbus et al. 2024 found +10% (Apple) and +17% (Fitbit) during initiation. 3 showed no change (Phillips et al.; Golbus et al. 2026; Klasnja et al. 2020). The listed MRT-specific caveats are carryover between decision points, missing data at decision points, and inconsistent reporting of distal outcomes. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1934024/full)
- A 2026 MRT of push notifications in **287 adults with hypertension** found no significant main effect on steps in the 60-min window, with a small gain only in a low-activity subgroup. **[secondary: reported via the Frontiers review table; primary JMIR paper not opened]** — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1934024/full)
- **Jaremba et al., *Scientific Reports* (Feb 2026)** re-analyzed a JITAI feasibility RCT for interpersonal distress (N = 77). They found **no significant proximal effects and high non-engagement with prompts**, and attribute this to timing and readiness design problems rather than to the content. — [Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-39518-z); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41673098/)
- **"Shift" MRT protocol (2026)**: 65 adults with post-trauma anger, self-report 4 times a day for 30 days, randomization to tailored modules. Acceptability is measured through surveys and engagement indices. This is a current template for an MRT protocol paper. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41865715/)
- **MRT methods for multi-arm alerts (2026)**: arXiv 2608.05135, "Micro-randomized Trials with Categorical Treatments and Binary Proximal Outcome: Causal Effect Estimation and Sample Size Calculation". It is directly relevant to comparing several alert wordings or modalities against no alert with a binary proximal outcome (e.g. "left area within 15 min"). **[title only]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.05135)
- **Cost of false alerts in older adults (2023, foundational)**: Tran et al., *Cardiology and Cardiovascular Medicine* 2023 (Pulsewatch RCT). Of 80 analyzed participants (mean age 64.3), 12% received ≥1 false AF alert, and **67% of everyone who received any AF alert received a false one**. Over 14 days, false alerts were associated with lower SF-12 physical health (β −7.53, p = 0.02) and lower chronic-symptom self-management self-efficacy (β −8.32, p = 0.004). The effect was **dose-dependent: >2 false alerts gave β −14.08 for physical health (p = 0.001)**, while 1–2 alerts showed no significant effect. GAD-7 anxiety change was not significant (p = 0.39). The analysis was unadjusted, small and short. — [PMC10358285](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10358285/)
- **Receptivity and timing (foundational)**: Künzler et al. (IMWUT 2019) define receptivity as interruptibility combined with engagement. They show that generic interruptibility research does not transfer directly to health interventions. — [Dartmouth PDF](https://kotz.cs.dartmouth.edu/research/kunzler-receptivity/kunzler-receptivity.pdf). A 2025 field preprint found afternoon and evening the most effective times, and a higher response when the **screen is on or the device unlocked**, which supports device state as a timing signal. — [arXiv 2508.02817](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.02817)
- Most interruptibility work uses physical context (location, activity). Cognitive context is under-used. — [arXiv 1711.10171 survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.10171)

### Inferences
- IRSA's core alert ("leave this crowded area") is a **high-stakes, event-triggered, location-conditioned JITAI**. No published JITAI evidence supports the assumption that such alerts change behaviour. The nearest data (activity JITAIs) show small proximal effects, and these decay or vanish in some MRTs.
- The Pulsewatch finding that harm appears only above about 2 false alerts suggests IRSA needs an **explicit false-alarm budget** (e.g. a cap on interruptive alerts per day or week) and conservative thresholds. Below the cap, it should use a tiered design: ambient information first, then an interruptive alert only when dose-rate and crowd estimates both exceed thresholds with stated confidence.
- A **micro-randomized trial is the right method** for IRSA's alert policy. Decision points would be entries into geofenced crowded venues or transport. Randomization would be alert vs no alert, or several alert variants, with a probability below 1 so the system stays safe. Proximal outcomes could be dwell time in the venue, a route change, and momentary distress (single-item EMA). The safety and ethics case is easier because existing paper instructions remain in force in the "no alert" arm. The 2026 categorical-MRT sample-size methods apply.
- IRSA should record **alert burden metrics** that the JITAI literature itself under-reports: alerts per day, dismissal and override rate, time to response, and opt-out or uninstall. These would address a gap acknowledged in the field, not just in IRSA.

### Gaps
- I found no CHI 2026, IMWUT 2026, CSCW 2026, MobiSys or SenSys 2026, or KDD 2026 paper on alert fatigue specifically for patient-facing health alerts. Searches returned older IMWUT receptivity work. The IMWUT 2026 tables of contents were not browsed.
- I found no JITAI or MRT on safety or protective alerts (as opposed to health promotion) that prompt people to leave a place, and none on radiation.
- The primary paper for the 287-person hypertension MRT was not opened. Its numbers come from the Frontiers review table.
- Nahum-Shani et al.'s foundational JITAI framework (2018) was not re-verified in this session.

## 2. LLM conversational agents grounded in wearable and sensor data

### Takeaway
The flagship 2026 result is Google's multi-agent **Personal Health Agent (PHA)**, published in *Nature* on 21 Oct 2026. It beat base Gemini, but clinicians still rated **17% of outputs as carrying at least mild potential harm (2% moderate to severe)**. Responses were about 6× slower than a single agent, and only conversation quality was evaluated, not behaviour or health outcomes. Independent 2026 benchmarks show LLMs are weak at numerical reasoning over real wearable time series: most models score below 60% on WearableQA. Taken together, this means IRSA should keep the dose and risk computation in deterministic code and use the LLM only to communicate.

### Cited Findings
- **The Anatomy of a Personal Health Agent**: preprint arXiv 2508.20148 (v1 27 Aug 2025; Heydari et al., 38 authors), published in *Nature* 21 Oct 2026. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20148); [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11161-8); [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/the-anatomy-of-a-personal-health-agent/)
  - Architecture: a data-science agent (time-series analysis), a health domain-expert agent, and a health-coach agent using a specified psychological strategy, all orchestrated together. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20148)
  - Evaluation: **10 benchmark tasks, >7,000 annotations, >1,100 hours** of effort from health experts and end users. Data came from an IRB-reviewed study of about 1,200 consenting Fitbit users with questionnaire and blood-test data. Design inputs included >1,300 real health queries, a survey of >500 users and an expert workshop. Data-science sub-agent: auto-evaluator trained on 354 analysis plans from 10 expert data scientists, plus 173 unit tests. Analysis-plan quality was **75.6% vs 53.7%** for the base model. — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/the-anatomy-of-a-personal-health-agent/)
  - Harm review: **83% no or minimal potential harm, 15% mild, 2% moderate to severe**. Clinicians rated 65% of domain-agent summaries useful or very useful vs 30% for the base model. **[secondary]** — [DistilINFO](https://distilinfo.com/2026/10/26/google-personal-health-ai-agent-nature/); [LongevityDocs](https://news.longevitydocs.org/research/personal-health-agent-multi-agent-ai-wearables-nature)
  - Mean response time was about **3.5 min vs 36 s** for a single-agent system. The domain agent got the correct leading diagnosis in **46.1% of 2,000** retrospective consumer cases. **[secondary; not verified in primary]** — [DistilINFO](https://distilinfo.com/2026/10/26/google-personal-health-ai-agent-nature/)
  - Coverage stresses that "everything measured is conversation quality and short-term task performance" and that there is no behaviour or health-outcome evidence. Alphabet funded the work and most authors are current or former Alphabet staff. — [LongevityDocs](https://news.longevitydocs.org/research/personal-health-agent-multi-agent-ai-wearables-nature)
  - Google describes PHA as a research prototype and a "conceptual framework for research purposes", not a product. The blog post lists no limitations or failure modes. — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/the-anatomy-of-a-personal-health-agent/)
- **PHIA (Personal Health Insights Agent)**: arXiv 2406.06464 (2024), later published in *Nature Communications* (2025/26). It reached **84% exact-match accuracy on objective numerical queries vs 74%** for a code-generation baseline. **[secondary figures]** — [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-67922-y); [arXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2406.06464)
- **WearableQA** (Lee et al., arXiv 2609.05405, 4 Sep 2026): 4,084 ten-option questions built from wearable time series, blood biomarkers and demographics of **200 real users with up to 500 days** of data each, in 16 question types. Across **14 LLMs, accuracy ranged from 19.6% to 72.9%** (chance 10%), and "most models achieve accuracies below 60%". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.05405v1)
  - Data reasoning (computation) was consistently harder than health reasoning, with gaps above 25 points for some models, and Python tool access gave large gains. **[secondary summary]** — [AI Weekly](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/wearableqa-benchmark-stretches-14-llms-from-196-to-729); [HF summary](https://github.com/vollero/hf-daily-paper-summaries/blob/main/summaries/2026/09/2026-09-10/2609.05405.md)
- **Older adults and LLM sensemaking of wearable data (2026)**: Dai et al., arXiv 2603.23733 (24 Mar 2026; venue not stated on arXiv, though an ACM DL entry exists). Seven-day diary study plus interviews with **8 CVD patients aged 64–82**. Themes include "navigating emotional complexity", "selective engagement with health metrics", "negotiating socio-technical dynamics of sharing" and "cautious optimism toward AI". Participants said data "feels overwhelming". Design calls: support emotional engagement, reinforce patient agency, acknowledge embodied experience, and use **expert-in-the-loop mechanisms for safety**. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23733); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3820253.3820281)
- CHI 2026 (Barcelona, 13–17 Apr 2026) included papers on older adults' preferences for explanations from LLM conversational AI ("Sometimes You Need Facts, and Sometimes a Hug"), an LLM mindfulness assistant for older adults, and older adults' Oura Ring sleep tracking. **[titles only, from proceedings front matter]** — [CHI '26 front matter](https://dl.acm.org/action/showFmPdf?doi=10.1145%2F3772318); see also [arXiv 2603.08164](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.08164) ("Tell Me Why, When, and How", older adults' perceptions of conversational-AI explanations)

### Inferences
- PHA is the best-resourced sensor-grounded agent to date and still produced at least mild potential harm in about 1 in 6 outputs. An IRSA built by a small IAEA TC team should therefore **not let an LLM compute dose, cumulative contact dose or risk classifications**. Those should come from deterministic, validated code. The LLM should only phrase, explain and answer clarifying questions, constrained by templates and retrieval from approved discharge instructions.
- WearableQA's main failure mode, numerical reasoning over noisy time series, is exactly IRSA's dose-rate stream task. Tool and code execution helps but does not remove the risk.
- PHA's evaluation template can be adapted for IRSA: task-level benchmarks, clinician harm-severity rating, end-user ratings and expert-hours accounting. The PHA critique also applies: IRSA should add **behavioural and safety outcomes** (measured dose to others, adherence to restrictions) and **psychological outcomes** (anxiety, stigma), not just conversation quality.
- PHA's 3.5-min latency is incompatible with a "real-time" alert. Alerts should not depend on a multi-agent LLM round trip. Generate them locally from rules and let the LLM handle follow-up dialogue.

### Gaps
- I could not open the *Nature* PHA full text (paywall redirect), so harm, latency and diagnosis numbers rely on secondary coverage.
- Google's PH-LLM (sleep and fitness coaching LLM, reported in *Nature Medicine* 2025) was **not verified in this session**.
- I did not research Apple, Oura or Whoop LLM coaches, or Google "SensorFM" (2026; seen only in a [Forbes headline](https://www.forbes.com/sites/josipamajic/2026/07/10/googles-sensorfm-reveals-where-ai-takes-wearable-health/)). No peer-reviewed evaluation of the commercial coaches was found.
- I found no paper on an LLM agent grounded in a dosimeter or any radiation sensor.

## 3. Older adults and patients with cancer using AI chatbots and wearables

### Takeaway
2025–2026 qualitative and survey evidence is consistent. Older users trust their own bodily sense and trusted people more than device data. Evaluative or alarming metrics can cause stress, and some older adults explicitly fear anxiety and "constant surveillance" from health wearables. Older cancer survivors are less likely to follow chatbot advice. Adherence takes weeks to develop. Radioiodine patients already report isolation and anxiety, which risk-framed "radiation" alerts could worsen. I found no study that measured stigma directly.

### Cited Findings
- **Lu et al., *JMIR mHealth and uHealth* 2026;14:e71509 (29 Apr 2026)**: interpretative phenomenological study of 18 older adults aged 66–82 (mean 72.1) using a real-time audio-feedback wearable for 3 weeks. An unexpected decline in metrics triggered stress, confusion and anxiety in some participants, and step tracking felt "competitive and stressful". Participants **trusted bodily awareness over device data** and relied on trusted professionals to interpret devices. Use was very uneven (6 limited, 7 moderate, 4 high), and adherence developed over extended time. Recommendations: avoid evaluative framing, let users choose which metrics they see, and tailor feedback to sensory abilities. — [PMC13173093](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13173093/)
- **Survey of 1,521 Portuguese adults aged 60–99** (*Discover Public Health*, published around Jan 2026): **54.9% were concerned about anxiety** from wearable health technology, and some respondents cited "constant surveillance". **[secondary: figure from search excerpt; full text not opened]** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12982-025-01271-5)
- **JMIR 2026 (30 Jul 2026, vol 28) qualitative study** of patients, caregivers and nurses on chatbots for patient education: limited digital health literacy and the digital divide among older patients were major barriers, with nurses seeing greater risk for complex conditions such as cancer. The authors warn **against treating older adults as one digitally disadvantaged group** and call for integration tailored to patient characteristics, information risk and context. **[secondary: search abstract]** — [JMIR doi:10.2196/87254](https://doi.org/10.2196/87254)
- **JMIR Cancer 2025 (e77390)**: US breast and prostate cancer survivors using AI conversational tools. **The older the participant, the less likely they were to follow chatbot advice.** Trust was higher for general questions. Digital literacy was not formally assessed. **[secondary: search abstract]** — [JMIR Cancer](https://cancer.jmir.org/2025/1/e77390)
- **Older adults with CVD (2026)**: self-tracking is "affective, interpretive, and socially situated", and data can feel "overwhelming". — [arXiv 2603.23733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23733)
- **False alerts in over-50s (2023)**: dose-dependent drop in perceived physical health and self-management confidence (see Section 1). — [PMC10358285](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10358285/)
- **Radioiodine patients' psychosocial experience (foundational, pre-2025)**:
  - Korean phenomenological study of thyroid cancer patients after radioiodine therapy. Theme clusters: "feeling social isolation", "struggling alone", "solitude in a crowd", "anxiety about death". Authors stress family support systems. — [PMC9052837](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9052837)
  - Isolation-ward feasibility study: anxiety and depression worsened after consent and improved about 40 days after treatment. Timed psychological support is recommended. — [PMC11318648](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11318648/)
  - Egyptian descriptive study: **81.5% reported depressed mood and anxiety during treatment**, and most needed support. — [EKB journals](https://journals.ekb.eg/article_232860.html)

### Inferences
- IRSA's phrasing "your dose rate … indicates high exposure risk to others, please leave this crowded area" frames the patient as a hazard in public. For a population that already reports "solitude in a crowd" and isolation, such alerts plausibly increase anxiety and self-stigma. Older users may also discount the alert in favour of their own sense of being well. Alert copy should be **co-designed and tested** with patients, e.g. neutral, action-first wording ("a quieter route is available"), no alarm sounds by default, and an explanation available on request.
- The concept paper says radioiodine patients are chosen "because they are younger", yet the persona is an older man. Both the evidence and that inconsistency call for **explicit stratified recruitment by age and digital literacy** (e.g. eHEALS or the Mobile Device Proficiency Questionnaire) and for caregiver involvement. The JMIR 2026 finding that older adults are not homogeneous argues against a single "older user" persona.
- Trusted-human mediation is a recurring finding: in Lu et al. 2026, and in the expert-in-the-loop recommendation of Dai et al. 2026. A nurse or medical-physicist contact channel and onboarding before discharge are likely to drive trust and adherence more than the chatbot alone.
- Adherence develops over weeks, but the radioiodine restriction period is short, typically days to a couple of weeks (this duration is domain knowledge, not sourced here). The onboarding window is therefore very short, so the app should be set up and rehearsed before discharge.

### Gaps
- I found no 2025–2026 study of **stigma** caused by radiation-related or contagion-like risk alerts. Stigma is a stated motivation in TB vDOT work but rarely a measured outcome (see Section 6).
- I found no study of radioiodine or radiopharmaceutical patients using wearables or apps.
- The age and sex profile of radioiodine patients (e.g. the proportion of women of working age) is **not sourced in these notes**. It should be checked against other researchers' epidemiology notes.

## 4. Privacy-preserving location and contact sensing: on-device LLMs, GAEN lessons, contact logging

### Takeaway
By late 2026, small language models running fully on a smartphone are demonstrated in research for health time-series reasoning, so cloud upload of dose and location traces is not technically necessary. The GAEN experience shows that a strictly decentralized, location-free design was still adopted by only a minority (19–49% in US states). Even so, where uptake was decent, as in England, it averted many cases. Privacy concerns, accuracy (false positives) and transparency drove adoption, and mandatory use was judged likely incompatible with human rights standards.

### Cited Findings
- **On-device SLMs for mobile health (2025–2026)**:
  - *PocketTSLM* (arXiv 2610.21308, Oct 2026): a time-series language model deployed entirely on a smartphone, with "no health data transmitted during inference". The authors note that HIPAA or GDPR applicability still depends on deployment context. **[abstract-level]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21308)
  - *On-Device Language Models for Privacy-Preserving Stress Prediction* (arXiv 2609.11961, Sep 2026): evaluates accuracy plus on-device latency and throughput on PMData (16 participants, 5 months). **[abstract-level]** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11961)
  - *HealthSLM-Bench* (arXiv 2509.07260, Sep 2025): benchmark of SLMs for mobile and wearable health monitoring. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07260)
  - *Menta* (arXiv 2512.02716, Dec 2025): an SLM for on-device mental-health prediction. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.02716)
- **GAEN and exposure-notification apps (foundational, 2020–2023)**:
  - GAO review of 11 US states: **adoption 19% to 49%**. States measured downloads, not sustained use. Officials named **privacy concerns as a common barrier despite privacy-by-design**. By design, states could not know who downloaded the app, who contacted whom, or who was notified. — [GAO-21-104622](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-21-104622); [GAO blog](https://www.gao.gov/blog/covid-19-exposure-notification-apps-are-available.-are-they-working)
  - Users cared about **accuracy (false positives and negatives)**, which may have influenced adoption as much as privacy did. — [Berkman Klein Center](https://medium.com/berkman-klein-center/accuracy-incentives-honesty-insights-from-covid-19-exposure-notification-apps-aa944664c844)
  - Effectiveness where uptake was substantial: in England and Wales (Wymant et al., *Nature* 2021), the NHS COVID-19 app averted about 284,000 cases (modelling) or about 594,000 (statistical, 95% CI 317,000–914,000) by Dec 2020. That is roughly one case averted per consenting index case, with each 1-point increase in uptake associated with a 0.8–2.3% reduction in cases. 1.7 million notifications were sent, with a secondary attack rate of 6.0%. — [Warwick WRAP / Nature 2021](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/153103); [Kendall accepted PDF](https://michellekendall.github.io/papers/Nature2021_accelerated.pdf). The first-year follow-up (Kendall et al., *Nature Communications* 2023) estimated about 1 million cases averted (sensitivity analysis 450k–1.4M), 44,000 hospital cases and 9,600 deaths averted. — [Nature Communications 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-36495-z.pdf)
  - A systematic review of the epidemiological effectiveness of EN apps found observed impact "extremely limited due to low app adoption and adherence". **[date and authors not verified; ResearchGate listing]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404630695_Epidemiological_Effectiveness_of_Exposure_Notification_Applications_Implemented_During_the_COVID-19_Pandemic_A_Systematic_Review)
  - **Ada Lovelace Institute**: the April 2020 rapid review warned that premature, ineffective apps risk undermining trust and that **mandatory use would "likely fall foul of the human rights standards"**. It called for primary legislation, **sunset clauses** and independent oversight. The Aug 2020 citizen deliberation found that lack of transparency (the Isle of Wight trial) generated distrust. The **2023 retrospective** found demonstrated feasibility and some positive impact, but "overall impact … unclear due to significant evidence gaps and lack of independent research". — [Exit through the App Store (2020)](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Ada-Lovelace-Institute-Exit-through-the-App-Store-Explainer-for-Government-April-2020.pdf); [Confidence in a crisis? (2020)](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Confidence-in-a-crisis-report_Ada-Lovelace-Institute_Aug-2020.pdf); [Answers in the App Store (2023)](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/Ada-Lovelace-Institute-policy-briefing-Answers-in-the-App-Store.pdf)
  - JMIR 2021: adoption of contact-tracing apps is a balance between privacy and effectiveness. — [JMIR 2021;23(3):e25726](https://www.jmir.org/2021/3/e25726/)

### Inferences
- IRSA's design is the **inverse of GAEN's privacy architecture**. It uses continuous GPS location, venue identity, named contacts (including minors such as grandchildren) and optional reporting to a regulator. GAEN deliberately avoided all of these and still struggled with adoption. Expect lower uptake and more abandonment unless IRSA defaults to **on-device processing, no raw location upload, and patient-held logs**.
- On-device SLMs are now technically feasible for the dialogue layer (research-grade in 2026), and deterministic dose logic needs no cloud at all. A privacy-by-default architecture is therefore realistic: crowd data fetched as coarse tiles or venue categories, matched locally, with nothing about the patient's trajectory leaving the phone.
- Contact logging of grandchildren involves **third-party and minors' data**. Better options are logging counts and durations without identities, or letting caregivers keep their own records. Contact dose estimates should be shown as ranges, given the uncertainty in proximity sensing.
- Accuracy drove EN adoption. IRSA's false-positive rate for crowd-risk alerts (Sections 1 and 5) will directly affect trust and continued use.

### Gaps
- I found no 2025–2026 paper specifically on **location privacy in health apps**, or on differential privacy or federated analytics for crowd estimation in a patient context. Searches returned on-device SLM papers but none applying DP to occupancy queries.
- The on-device SLM papers were seen only at abstract level, and their peer-review status is unclear.
- EFF material on contact tracing was not retrieved beyond its 2020 quarantine-surveillance post (Section 6).

## 5. Reliability of crowd and occupancy data (Google Popular Times and live busyness, OpenStreetMap, mobile network data), especially in LMICs

### Takeaway
None of IRSA's proposed crowd sources gives validated absolute occupancy. Google Popular Times is a **relative-to-own-peak** index derived from opted-in Location History users, shown only for venues with enough such users, with an unpublished method and only small, informal validations. OpenStreetMap building coverage is very unequal: below 20% completeness for cities holding 48% of the world's urban population, and about 9% in South Asia. Mobile-network data correlate poorly with population density in at least one LMIC study (Senegal). Coverage will be weakest exactly where many IAEA TC recipients are.

### Cited Findings
- **Google Popular Times and live busyness (official description)**: computed from "aggregated and anonymized data from users who have opted in to Google Location History". It **appears only for businesses with enough visits from such users**, owners cannot add it, and each hour is shown **relative to the venue's typical weekly peak**. Live data are compared to the venue's "usual" activity. No numeric thresholds or methods are published. — [Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/6263531)
- **Validation studies are few and small**:
  - Donostia-San Sebastián: Popular Times correlated with traffic volumes (R² > 0.85) on primary links, but predictive power weakened on other links and across seasons and day types. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/323176011_Can_Google_Maps_Popular_Times_Be_an_Alternative_Source_of_Information_to_Estimate_Traffic-Related_Impacts)
  - Informal comparison with Placemeter sensor counts: Google showed one weekday peak (6 pm) where sensors showed two (morning and evening). Data had to be screen-captured because it cannot be exported. **[not peer-reviewed]** — [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/how-accurate-googles-popular-time-function-m%C3%A9lissa-sanchot)
- **OpenStreetMap building completeness** (Herfort et al., *Nature Communications* 2023): ML estimate across 13,189 urban agglomerations. Only 1,848 urban centres (16% of the urban population) exceed 80% completeness, while **9,163 cities (48% of the urban population) are below 20%**. Regional averages: South Asia 9%, Middle East & North Africa 12%, Latin America & Caribbean 20%, East Asia & Pacific 20%. Gini coefficient 0.8. Headline global figures differ between versions of the work (21% vs 38%). — [Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-39698-6); [PMC10326063](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10326063/); [SotM 2022 talk](https://media.ccc.de/v/state-of-the-map-2022-academic-track-18598-inequalities-in-the-completeness-of-openstreetmap-buildings-in-urban-centers)
  - OSM coverage is best in high-HDI countries. — [Scientific Data 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-025-06132-z). Sparse in Africa and South America. — [GlobalBuildingAtlas arXiv 2506.04106](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.04106). Google Open Buildings (2016–2023 temporal dataset) is an ML-derived alternative for the Global South, but it gives footprints, not building use. — [PMC13230486](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13230486)
  - Slum mapping in Africa and Asia reached up to 84% completeness at some sites and none at some Asian sites. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350874724_Analysis_of_OpenStreetMap_Data_Quality_at_Different_Stages_of_a_Participatory_Mapping_Process_Evidence_from_Slums_in_Africa_and_Asia)
- **Mobile-network (CDR) data**: in Senegal (PLOS ONE 2020), the correlation between average phone activity and population density "may be insufficiently high to provide an accurate representation". Operator market share varies by region and is a key error source. — [PMC7326166](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7326166/); [arXiv 1907.04812](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.04812v1). Deville et al. (PNAS 2014) matched census accuracy in Portugal and France, with precision depending on tower density. — [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2014PNAS..11115888D); [Southampton](https://www.southampton.ac.uk/news/2014/10/28-accuracy-of-mobile-phone-population-mapping.page)

### Inferences
- An alert such as "the surrounding crowd indicates high exposure risk" needs **absolute** people-per-area near the patient, at room or carriage scale, for minutes at a time. Popular Times offers a venue-level relative index with an unknown error. OSM tags (where present) give building type, not occupancy. CDR data give cell-level counts that require operator agreements and are poorly calibrated in LMICs. The crowd component would therefore be the **largest source of false alerts** and should be presented as coarse, uncertain context ("this kind of place is usually busy at this hour"), not as a measured exposure.
- In many TC recipient countries, coverage gaps mean IRSA would silently fail to warn (false negatives) in poorly mapped areas while over-warning in well-mapped ones. That could create inequity, and it should be stated as a known limitation.
- A more robust and privacy-friendlier approach is to rely mainly on **patient-reported context** (e.g. "Are you on a bus? About how many people are within 2 m?") together with venue category. The conversational agent already in the concept could ask this. Data-driven crowd feeds would then be an optional enhancement in countries where validated.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed validation of Google **live busyness** accuracy, and no study of Popular Times coverage by country or income group.
- Unverified: whether Google's Dec 2023 shift of Timeline/Location History to on-device storage has affected Popular Times data volume or coverage. This was **not checked**.
- I found no study of real-time indoor occupancy estimation (Wi-Fi, BLE) at public-transport or clinic scale in LMICs.
- I did not research licensing terms of the Google Places API for medical or safety use.

## 6. Stigma, autonomy and "surveillance" framing: TB video-DOT, quarantine-tracking apps, and implications for regulator reporting

### Takeaway
Remote monitoring is widely accepted when the patient experiences it as convenience, privacy and empowerment: patients preferred video-DOT over in-person DOT in 21 of 22 studies, and 92–93% accepted or were satisfied with it. The same kind of technology, imposed by authorities as compliance surveillance (COVID quarantine wristbands and apps), drew strong human-rights objections, worries about normalizing future surveillance, and documented wrongful penalties from technical errors. IRSA's optional regulator reporting for "non-compliance" detection sits on the surveillance side of this line unless it is redesigned.

### Cited Findings
- **Video DOT for TB**:
  - Systematic review of patient perceptions: in **21 of 22 studies, patients preferred or accepted vDOT over in-person DOT**. Drivers were cost and time savings, a **personal sense of empowerment**, convenience and **privacy**. — [PMC10877938](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10877938/); [PubMed 38380432](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38380432)
  - 2025 scoping review (22 articles): 92% of patients very satisfied, with limited evidence on effectiveness. — [PubMed 40330177](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40330177/)
  - 2025 Swiss prospective study: **92.7% acceptance** among invited patients. VOT is framed as "less stigmatising" than DOT, but stigma is a motivation, not a measured outcome. — [Swiss Medical Weekly](https://smw.ch/index.php/smw/article/view/4238)
  - CDC 2023 recommends vDOT as equivalent to in-person DOT. — [MMWR via PMC10042619](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10042619/)
- **COVID quarantine-tracking apps and wristbands (2020–2021)**:
  - Examples: Poland (app with random selfie check-ins), Hong Kong (StayHomeSafe app plus mandatory wristband), Bahrain (mandatory electronic bracelet), Kazakhstan. — [ICNL](https://www.icnl.org/post/analysis/covid-19-the-surveillance-pandemic); [ASEAN Post](https://theaseanpost.com/article/electronic-wristbands-help-covid-19-fight)
  - Concerns: discriminatory application against vulnerable groups, and a "Trojan Horse" that normalizes future surveillance. Moscow's quarantine app technical flaws reportedly led to **wrongful fines of hundreds, perhaps thousands, of people**. Effectiveness "might be impossible to evaluate fully due to the lack of collected data". **[secondary: from search excerpts of advocacy analyses; Moscow figure not checked against a primary report]** — [ICNL](https://www.icnl.org/post/analysis/covid-19-the-surveillance-pandemic); [World Justice Project](https://worldjusticeproject.org/sites/default/files/documents/Surveillance%20COVID_v4.pdf)
  - EFF argued for COVID-19 patients' right to privacy against quarantine surveillance. — [EFF 2020](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2020/05/covid-19-patients-right-privacy-against-quarantine-surveillance)
  - Ada Lovelace Institute: mandatory use would likely breach human rights standards. It recommended primary legislation, sunset clauses and independent oversight. — [Ada Lovelace 2020](https://www.adalovelaceinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Ada-Lovelace-Institute-Exit-through-the-App-Store-Explainer-for-Government-April-2020.pdf)
- **Older users' own surveillance concerns**: Portuguese adults aged 60+ cited "constant surveillance" among their concerns about wearable health tech. **[secondary]** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12982-025-01271-5). Older adults with CVD "negotiate socio-technical dynamics of sharing" health data. — [arXiv 2603.23733](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23733)

### Inferences
- The vDOT evidence shows that acceptability depends on **who controls the data and what the patient gains**: less travel, privacy from neighbours, a sense of agency. Regulator reporting of location and activity to detect "non-compliance" offers the patient no direct benefit and casts them as a suspect. It is likely to depress uptake among all users, including those who decline it, because the app's mere capability to report can deter adoption. That parallels the GAEN privacy-concern finding in Section 4.
- Quarantine-app experience shows **error-driven harms**, such as wrongful fines from technical flaws. If IRSA's crowd and dose inference has high false-positive rates (Section 5), "non-compliance" flags would be unreliable evidence, and acting on them could be unjust.
- Defensible alternatives:
  - drop regulator reporting from v1;
  - if it is kept, make it **patient-initiated and aggregate**, e.g. an end-of-restriction self-attestation or summary ("estimated contact dose within guidance: yes/no") rather than location traces;
  - separate the clinical-support function from any enforcement function, with different data stores;
  - apply sunset and deletion at the end of the restriction period;
  - add independent ethics and DPIA review, following Ada Lovelace's principles.
- Stigma and anxiety need **measurement**: perceived-stigma scales adapted from TB/HIV instruments, STAI or GAD-7 before and after alerts, and qualitative interviews. The TB literature shows that stigma reduction is often assumed rather than measured.

### Gaps
- I found no study of electronic monitoring for **radiation-protection compliance** of nuclear-medicine patients, and no regulator-side evaluation of such reporting.
- I found no systematic, peer-reviewed effectiveness evaluation of quarantine wristbands or apps.
- vDOT stigma outcomes are rarely measured directly (Section 3 gap).
- CSCW 2026 papers on patient monitoring or "care vs control" framing were not searched individually.
